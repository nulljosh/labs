# Registry arithmetic looks open, one runtime figure is old

No registry-wide arithmetic audit of ClinicalTrials.gov results turned up in the sources searched, so the three registry observations have no direct precedent there. The methods behind them are old, though. The SE-to-SD conversion is in the Cochrane Handbook, and a CI-versus-p-value recalculation ran on 196,278 MEDLINE abstracts in 2018, which is the closest precedent for the sign-slip candidate. On the Busy Beaver side, the second-longest 5-state runtime (23,554,764) is already printed in Michel's 2009 survey, so it is not new. The 5-state step sum is a fifth term for an OEIS sequence that has existed since August 2025 and stops at n=4, so it looks open as far as these sources show. The distinct-runtime counts 79 and 1,207 appear in no source searched, and they answer a question Aaronson asked in print. Every verdict here is "not found in sources searched," which is a narrower claim than "proven new."

## Registry checks: the method is standard, the application is not found

The registry runs checks, but the public record says little about their content. A 2017 Peer Review Congress abstract by Dobbins and colleagues at NLM says results are validated through structured elements, automated system checks and manual staff review. In a convenience sample of 215 submissions, **52 (24%) had internal inconsistency** flagged as a major issue, and that finding was made by staff ([Peer Review Congress](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/characterizing-major-issues-in-clinicaltrials-gov-results-submissions/)). The 2021 SHACL study ran **15 rules over all 355,862 studies** and reports that none of them tests statistical values, dispersion, CIs or p-values ([Keuchel and Spicher](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/doi/10.3233/SHTI210183)). So the automated public checks are structural. Any arithmetic checks NLM runs internally are not published in what I could open. "Not found" is the right phrase, because the posted record shows what got past QC, not what QC tests.

Candidate 1a rests on a textbook rule. For a group mean, the Cochrane Handbook gives SD = SE times the square root of n. For large samples, the SD also comes from the CI width: divide by 3.92, then multiply by the square root of n. The Handbook says the formula does not apply to differences between groups ([Cochrane Handbook, archived v5.0.0](https://training-noproxy.cochrane.org/handbook/archive/v5.0.0/chapter_7/7_7_3_2_obtaining_standard_deviations_from_standard_errors_and.htm)). So the novelty, if it holds, lies in running the rule across posted outcome tables. The false-positive risks are real. A CI on a between-arm difference, a CI on a log-scale ratio, a CI at a level the registry field does not state, and small-sample t values can all look like SE labels. The 63 reverse cases are the first place to look for these.

Candidate 1b rests on one inequality. The total sum of squares is at least the sum of the within-arm sums, so the Total SD cannot fall below the pooled within-arm SD. An SEM in an SD field pushes the arm values far enough to break that bound. GRIMMER is the nearest method, but it tests only integer data ([Anaya 2016 preprint](https://peerj.com/preprints/2400)), and most outcome data are not integers. Before calling a Total infeasible, check that its N equals the sum of the arm Ns. Missing-data handling can break that equality without any labelling error. Literature on SD and SEM misuse in papers is large. A 2014 survey of 441 cardiovascular articles found that **64% had at least one incorrect SEM use** ([PMC4212967](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4212967/)), but that is a paper survey, not a registry check.

## Sign slips: a 2018 MEDLINE check already tests CI against p

Candidate 2 has the closest precedent. Georgescu and Wren extracted **486,318 ratio, CI and p-value items from 196,278 abstracts** across 5,652 journals, then recalculated the p-values from the reported CIs. They flagged **about 1,179 P-values (1.44%)** that would change the significance verdict at 0.05, and ratios that fell outside their own CI in **0.4% to 0.6%** of items ([Georgescu and Wren 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5946902/)). They did not run a dedicated sign check. A CI with the wrong sign and a p-value that disagrees sits in the class they test. So the sign framing and the registry source are the parts that look new, not the idea of testing CI against p.

Two cautions apply. Trial reports often pair an adjusted CI with an unadjusted or stratified p-value, so a disagreement is not automatically an error. Also, 61 analyses is a count, not a rate. The denominator matters: how many analyses in the registry have both a CI and a p-value posted. Statcheck, the best-known consistency tool, works on test statistics and degrees of freedom in psychology papers ([Nuijten et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26497820/)), and a Campbell Collaboration study applied it to systematic reviews ([PMC8428001](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8428001/)). Neither operates on registry CIs. Registry-scale statistics do exist. Adda, Decker and Ottaviani analysed posted primary-outcome p-values from phase II and III trials to study bunching at 0.05 ([arXiv 1907.00185](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.00185)). That work is a distribution study, not a consistency check. It does show that posted p-values are analysed at scale, so the feasibility of candidate 2 is not in doubt.

## Busy Beaver numbers: one is old, one is a new OEIS term

The runner-up runtime is old. Michel's 2009 survey lists the 5-state Marxen-Buntrock machine at 47,176,870 steps, and its Section 5.2.2, "Marxen and Buntrock's runner-up," gives **s = 23,554,764 and σ = 4097** for the second machine ([arXiv 0906.3749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.3749)). Aaronson's 2020 survey gives the gap to the champion as 23,622,106, which he states conditionally on Conjecture 10, saying it "appears to be" that value. Whether 23,554,764 is the second-longest of all halting 5-state machines is a separate claim. The 2025 proof enumerates all 181,385,789 5-state machines ([arXiv 2509.12337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12337)), so that claim can be checked from the public data. I found no source that states the checked result. The value is old, and only the full-enumeration confirmation could be new.

The step sum is the claim with the clearest open status. OEIS A386859 was submitted by Carlos Moya Garcia and approved on August 5, 2025. Its data stop at n=4: **32, 13504, 13141792, 23076152576** ([A386859](https://oeis.org/A386859)). The entry bounds the sum by max(A004147(n), A060843(n)) and their product. The candidate value 64,068,865,333,248 sits inside those bounds, which run from about 2.7 times 10 to the 13th to about 1.3 times 10 to the 21st. The average steps per halting machine rise smoothly: 1.00, 1.38, 1.74, 2.07, and about 2.4 for n=5. That is consistency, not validation. Because the full 5-state classification is public, anyone with that data could have computed the fifth term without publishing it. The defensible claim is that it is not in OEIS as of this check.

The distinct-runtime counts follow from the published bounds at small n, and the open values are at n=4 and n=5. For n=1 to 3, the Lazy Beaver values (2, 7, 22 in [A337805](https://oeis.org/A337805)) sit one above the busy beaver values (1, 6, 21). Every runtime below the Lazy Beaver value is attained, so the counts are 1, 6 and 21. These are derivations, not published counts. For n=4, the Lazy Beaver value is 72 and the busy beaver value is 107. Runtimes 1 to 71 are all attained, so 79 needs exactly **8 attained values between 73 and 107**. Michel's 4-state table lists six distinct values in that range: 78, 83, 84, 96, 97 and 107 ([arXiv 0906.3749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0906.3749)). So 79 is consistent with the table and needs two more values to be found. For n=5, the Lazy Beaver value is 427, so runtimes 1 to 426 are all attained, and 1,207 needs 781 more values between 428 and 47,176,870. Published data do not constrain that. A count for n=4 or n=5 would be the first published value for the R(n) entry, as far as these sources show.

A convention trap also matters. [A119683](https://oeis.org/A119683) counts runs that start and halt on a blank tape, with values 1, 4, 12, 34, 187 ([Hercher, arXiv 2510.17438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17438)). That is a different model, and 187 is not comparable to the 5-state busy beaver value. Any check should use halting from a blank tape with the A060843 step convention. At two symbols the wiki's 5-state halting count matches A004147, so the machine counts are consistent across sources ([bbchallenge](https://wiki.bbchallenge.org/wiki/Number_of_Halting_TMs)).

## What these searches cannot rule out

Several sources were not searched in depth. I did not check Data Colada, Retraction Watch, medRxiv full text or Google Scholar. PubMed returned a browser verification page, so the PubMed searches were limited to what other tools surfaced. The NLM Results QC criteria and the PRS validation rule list were not retrieved. I did not read Georgescu and Wren's full text, only the PMC summary. The bbchallenge Discord was not reachable, and Ligocki's blog was checked by post title only. The registry counts (304 and 63, 71 and 42, 61 and 52) are not in the research notes folder and were not checked here. Treat them as Noema's own observations.

Each verdict could flip on specific evidence. A CT.gov or any-database audit of SD/SE labels, or of CI-versus-p arithmetic on registry results, would weaken candidates 1 and 2. An NLM QC document that lists CI or SD arithmetic checks would do the same. A bbchallenge or Ligocki post with R(4) or R(5) counts would settle candidate 3b. A new A386859 term or a published 5-state step sum would settle candidate 3c.

Before any claim goes out, three checks are cheap. First, reproduce A386859(4) = 23,076,152,576 and the 5-state halting count from the pipeline, then compute the n=5 sum, so the enumeration is validated against a known term. Second, enumerate R(4) from the public halting list and see whether it gives 79. Third, for the registry checks, state the denominators and review a sample of the 304 and the 63 against the publications, and confirm which CIs are on differences or ratios.

## Conclusion

The registry candidates are a new application of old arithmetic, and the open question is the registry denominators and false-positive review, which I could not verify. The arithmetic is not the contribution. The contribution would be running it on a public record that has no public arithmetic audit. The same test applies to the claim that candidate 2 is new. Its framing as a sign check is narrow, and the 2018 MEDLINE work already runs the wider CI-versus-p test.

On the Busy Beaver side, the claims most likely to be new are the step sum and the 79 and 1,207 counts. Both can be checked by reproduction from public enumeration data, so they can be settled without a literature search. The 23,554,764 figure should not appear as a finding. It is in a 2009 table, and only the claim that it is the confirmed second-longest halting run is open.
