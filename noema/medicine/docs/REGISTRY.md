# ClinicalTrials.gov checks

These studies check posted results on ClinicalTrials.gov against themselves. `registry.py` reads baseline tables and `analyses.py` reads outcome analyses.

## Do registry Totals add up?

ClinicalTrials.gov posts a baseline table for every trial with results: each arm's count, mean and SD, then a Total column. `registry.py fetch` pulls every trial with results. 55,075 post a mean with an SD, and `registry.py` checks 71,214 Totals against their arms. None of the Totals falls outside the range of its arm means. The registry may check that itself.

The Total should be the mean weighted by arm size. 2,154 miss that by more than ten roundings. Missing values may explain many of them, because the posted counts are not always the people measured. But 328 Totals, in 234 trials, land to the last digit on the plain average of the arm means. That is right only when the arms are the same size, and luck would give about 27. A decoy test agrees. Take the point as far from the weighted mean as the plain average, on the other side. Missing data should push Totals to either side alike. Where both points fit inside the arm range, 283 Totals land on the plain average and 11 on the decoy. In 168 of the 328 the Total SD is also the plain average of the arm SDs and falls outside every pooled SD the arms allow. These look like Totals typed in as a spreadsheet AVERAGE of the arms.

To be safe, the clear set keeps only trials whose arms differ at least twofold in size and carry no note about missing data: 134 Totals in 107 trials, listed in `registry-baseline.json`. In one, arms of 8 and 18 people have mean BMIs of 27.7 and 33.53. The Total is 30.615, the plain average; weighted, it is 31.74.

Head counts behave. Across all 79,533 trials with results, every arm count adds up to its Total, 435,201 cells without one miss, so the registry likely adds count Totals itself while mean Totals are typed by hand. Sex, age group, race and ethnicity add past their arm's size in only 49 rows of 22 trials, and 41 of those are exactly double, the shape of a crossover trial that counts each person in both periods.

Total SDs can be checked without any counts. However the arms pool, with at least two people each, the Total's SD is at least 0.7 times the smallest arm SD and at most the largest arm SD widened by the spread of the arm means. `registry.py` flags a Total SD under half the smallest arm SD, or over twice the widest pooling allows, when the Total mean itself sits inside the arms. 407 of 70,923 such Totals, in 296 trials, fail, so no table like them can be right. 24 print an SD of 0. 42 sit near the arm SD divided by the square root of N, which is a standard error. 3 repeat the Total mean and 3 the head count. In others the arms look like the slip: arms of 11 and 12 people with VO2peak SDs of 1.0 and 0.9 beside a Total SD of 3.1. One trial gives ages of 43.6 and 31.9 with SDs of 9.5 and 10, then a Total SD of .17. Three flagged trials were fetched again live and match cell for cell. The rows are in `registry-sd.json`. The checker cannot say which cell is wrong, only that one is.

For the 165 Totals that are too wide, one cell can often be named. Read each arm's spread as a standard error and turn it back into an SD, by multiplying by the square root of the arm's count. Then 71 of them, in 42 trials, fit the arms exactly. Decoy factors do not: half, 0.7, 1.4 and twice that root fit 3, 12, 7 and 4, and Totals shuffled between trials fit 7. So in those trials the arms were almost surely posted as standard errors in a field labelled SD, and only the Total is a real SD. One linked paper says so. NCT01786109 posts ages of 43.2, 36.1 and 43.7 with spreads of 2.5, 2.5 and 2.1. Its paper (PMID 21803011) prints the same table under "Mean ± SEM". `registry.arms_as_sem` finds them, and the rows are under `arm_sem` in `registry-sd.json`. Journals are known to swap SEM for SD (Nagele, 2003, and later reviews). One search found no count of it in the registry.

Two literature searches found studies of missing and inconsistent registry fields, but none that checks the Total column this way, and no sign of how the registry validates Totals.

## Do registry confidence intervals have the right signs?

Results pages also post analyses: an estimate, a 95% interval and often a p-value. `analyses.py fetch` pulls all 329,322 of them. 126,313 are differences (mean, LS mean, risk, slope) with a two-sided interval, and 119,013 of those sit symmetric about their estimate, as most such intervals do. Some that do not become symmetric when one limit changes sign, like a mean difference of -21.6 with an interval of -23.52 to 19.58, where -19.58 fits. Lopsided intervals can be honest, so the p-value decides. In 61 analyses, in 52 trials, the posted interval disagrees with the posted p about zero and the flipped one agrees: -21.6 (-23.52, 19.58) with p below 0.0001. In 28 the p sides with the posted interval instead, which is about how often luck makes a flip look right. Intervals symmetric on the log scale are ratios under a difference label and are left out. Three flagged trials were fetched again live and match cell for cell. The rows are in `registry-ci-sign.json`. In 39 a limit that should be negative is printed positive, most often the upper limit of a negative difference (31). In 22 a limit picks up a stray minus, usually copied from a negative estimate onto an upper limit that crosses zero (20). The trials link 116 papers in PubMed, 71 of them open access. Two print the flipped interval: NCT00288912 reports 0.6 points lower (CI, -1.0 to -0.2; P = 0.007) where the registry has -1.0 to 0.2, and NCT03192176 prints -4.06 to -0.76 where the registry has 0.76. One paper, for NCT04636528, carries the slip too: -1.1 (-1.5 to 0.6) with P < .001, and -0.6 (0.2 to 1.4) with P = .12. A literature search found registry-versus-paper discrepancy studies, and Wren and Georgescu (2018) checking ratios against intervals in abstracts, but none on sign slips in registry analyses.

A caveat, checked. Some P values in the registry are one-sided. Among superiority analyses whose interval and exact P value clash about zero, where the interval itself implies a P between 0.004 and 0.3, 60 posted P values sit on one minus half the interval's P. That is the one-sided P for the other direction. Chance or other readings fit at most 2 each. 17 say Bayesian or one-sided in the method. Of the other 43, in 26 trials, three were read live: two say "one-sided" only in a free-text comment, and one says nothing. The field has no place to say a P value is one-sided, so a reader of the table alone would take these for two-sided. None of them is among the sign slips above.

## Are the spreads what their labels say?

Each outcome table on ClinicalTrials.gov labels its ± column as a standard deviation or a standard error, and many come with a confidence interval for the difference between two arms. The arms decide how wide that interval should be. For SDs, the half-width is about 1.96 times the root of s1²/n1 + s2²/n2. For standard errors it is about 1.96 times the root of s1² + s2². With at least 10 people per arm the two predictions sit far apart, so the interval itself says which one the column holds. `analyses.py fetch-arms` pulls every analysed outcome with a plain one-row table, 101,234 of them, and `analyses.py spreads` checks each two-arm interval against both predictions, within a quarter either way.

Of 7,685 SD-labelled intervals that fit one of them, 292, in 90 trials, fit standard errors instead. Of 16,878 SE-labelled ones, 60 fit SDs. An interval is skipped when its estimate is more than three times the difference between the arms, since then the two are not on the same scale. This is not noise at the edges. Bin the SD-labelled intervals by how far they sit from the standard-error prediction, in halvings. The counts run 268, 38 and 65 coming in, then 256 right on it, then 13 and 3 past it. A label slip puts them on the spot, and chance would not. One example is NCT02289963, a percent change in non-HDL cholesterol at 24 weeks. Arms of 100 and 97 give spreads of 2.2 and 2.3, labelled SD, beside an interval of -60.6 to -47.8. As SDs, that interval is five times too wide. As standard errors, it fits. Three flagged trials were fetched again live and match cell for cell. The rows are in `registry-spread.json`. Adjusted models can narrow an interval, but not by the factor of root n that separates the two readings.

Papers, for a seeded sample of 20 of the 97 trials. Six papers in PubMed Central print a flagged outcome. Two of them label the spread a standard error, as the interval predicted. NCT02856113 (PMID 40032809) prints the HbA1c changes as 0.09 (0.28) and -0.01 (0.28), and its footnote reads "LS mean SE". NCT03163264 (PMID 40892413) gives the "mean (SE) weight change". The registry calls both SDs. One paper says SD and is right, so that flag is a false one. NCT00945100 (PMID 23755872) posts its arms in logMAR but the difference in lines of acuity, ten times larger, so the width test compared different units. One paper gives only an interval, and two do not print the arm values. The other 14 trials have no full text in PubMed Central. That case led to the scale rule above, and it is no longer flagged.

Papers, second pass: every one of the other 74 flagged trials. 53 link a paper and 40 have one in PubMed Central; 14 of those print the flagged arm values and say what the spread is. 13 call it a standard error, in a column head or a footnote: "least squares mean ± SE" (NCT00846365), "Trough FEV1 mean (SE)" (NCT01040403), "mixed model estimates ± SE" (NCT02346136), "LS mean (s.e.m.)" (NCT03188666) and so on, each with the registry's numbers. The 14th, NCT04581200 (PMID 38957856), says SD and is right, but its interval, 0.02 (-0.11 to 0.15), is not the registry's 0.09 (-0.57 to 0.74), so the flag still marks a wrong cell. Across both passes, 15 of the 16 papers that can be read call the spread a standard error, and the 16th shows a different registry error. Trials, PMIDs and verdicts are in `registry-spread.json` under `paper_check`.
